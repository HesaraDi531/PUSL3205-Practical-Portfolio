# Lab 02 – AI in Everyday Challenges: The Busy Student

**Module:** PUSL3205 – Large Language Models and Generative AI  
**Laboratory session:** 02 – AI in Everyday Challenges  
**Assigned group:** Group 5  
**Topic:** The Busy Student  
**Activity type:** Group-based LLM product-development and problem-solving activity

## 1. Laboratory Session and Objective

The objective of this practical was to explore how a Large Language Model (LLM) can assist with a real-life challenge faced by university students. The activity encouraged critical thinking, creativity, problem-solving and collaboration by treating an LLM as a product-development partner rather than simply as a tool for answering questions.

Our group was assigned **Group 5 – The Busy Student**, which concerns managing lectures, assignments, group projects, independent study, extracurricular activities, personal time and competing deadlines. The exercise asked us to think about a product that could help students organise their responsibilities and manage time more effectively.

## 2. Understanding the Assigned Problem

University students often have commitments that overlap or change throughout the week. A student may need to attend lectures, complete individual assignments, coordinate with group members and prepare for assessments while maintaining time for personal activities. Without a clear plan, it can become difficult to decide what to do first, notice approaching deadlines or allocate sufficient time for important work.

This exercise highlighted that the problem is not simply forgetting dates. It also involves **prioritisation**, **realistic scheduling**, **workload awareness** and **balancing academic and personal responsibilities**. A useful solution should address these underlying needs rather than relying only on repeated reminders.

## 3. Tasks and Learning Process

The lab sheet asked each group to use an LLM as a product-development partner and consider five product-design elements: **product concept, name, key features, target users and visual prototype/mock-up**. It also specified a short group presentation explaining the problem, proposed product, development process and role of LLMs.

For our assigned scenario, the product-design thinking centred on the needs of busy university students. Possible features to consider include a combined timetable and deadline view, task prioritisation, study-session planning and reminders. These are **design possibilities for the scenario**, not a claim that a working application was developed.

The main learning process was to understand the student problem first, explore possible solutions with LLM support, assess which ideas would be genuinely useful, and consider how a proposed interface could communicate those features clearly. This README records the learning from that activity, as instructed for our portfolio submission.

## 4. What I Learned

### 4.1 Applying an LLM to a Real-World Problem

I learned that an LLM can support more than text generation. It can help organise a problem, suggest alternative approaches and turn a broad challenge into smaller design questions. However, the usefulness of its suggestions depends on whether the original problem and target user are clearly defined.

### 4.2 The Importance of Prompt Clarity

I learned why prompts should include context, a specific objective and relevant constraints. A request such as “suggest an app for students” is broad, whereas a prompt explaining that students must manage overlapping lectures, assignment deadlines and group work gives the LLM a clearer basis for suggestions. Follow-up questions are also useful for challenging ideas and identifying features that may be unnecessary or difficult to use.

### 4.3 Thinking from the User's Perspective

I learned that identifying target users is a key step in product development. Students have different timetables, study habits and workloads. A time-management product should therefore be understandable, practical and flexible rather than assuming that every student follows the same routine.

### 4.4 Evaluating AI-Generated Ideas Critically

An LLM can produce many feature suggestions, but more features do not automatically make a better product. I learned the importance of examining feasibility, simplicity and actual user benefit before selecting an idea. For example, excessive reminders could become another source of distraction for already busy students.

### 4.5 Collaboration and Communication

The group-based exercise reinforced the value of discussing different ideas and explaining why a particular solution might address the assigned problem. It also showed the importance of presenting the problem, intended users and proposed approach clearly, instead of focusing only on technical features.

## 5. Observations and Limitations

One important observation was that effective time management involves deciding **which tasks matter most**, not only recording tasks in a calendar. This suggests that prioritisation and manageable study plans may be just as valuable as deadlines and notifications.

The exercise also highlighted limitations of LLM-assisted product planning. AI suggestions may be generic, unrealistic or insufficiently aware of individual students' circumstances. An LLM cannot confirm whether a product idea is genuinely useful without feedback from potential users. A real implementation would also need to consider privacy if it stored students' schedules, personal commitments or academic information.

## 6. Learning Outcomes

Through this laboratory activity, I developed a better understanding of how to:

- Apply LLMs as assistants in early-stage problem-solving and product planning.
- Define an everyday student problem in terms of user needs.
- Formulate clearer prompts and assess generated suggestions rather than accepting them automatically.
- Consider product concepts, target users, features and interface design together.
- Recognise practical limitations, including usability, feasibility and data privacy.
- Communicate and reflect on ideas developed during a group activity.

## 7. Conclusion

Lab 02 demonstrated how LLMs can support creative problem-solving in a familiar university setting. The **Busy Student** scenario showed that a useful product must begin with a clear understanding of students' competing responsibilities. The most important learning for me was that AI-generated suggestions are a starting point for discussion, not a substitute for human judgement. Effective use of an LLM requires clear prompts, critical evaluation and attention to the needs of the people who would use the proposed solution.
